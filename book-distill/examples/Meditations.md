# Meditations — Marcus Aurelius

> *Private notebook of a Stoic philosopher-emperor, written in the field at Carnuntum and on the Quadi frontier. The thesis: nothing external can harm the rational soul, so the whole task of living is to keep the inner ruling faculty awake, just, sociable, and content with what nature sends — including its own dissolution.*

> Translation: George Long (1862), via Project Gutenberg. Includes Long's introduction, the twelve books, his appendix on the Fronto correspondence, and editorial notes.

---

## Key Ideas

### Judgment, not the event, is what wounds you
External objects stand mute outside the mind; what produces suffering is the opinion the mind adds to a bare impression. "He speaks ill of me" is the report; "I am harmed" is the addition. Wipe off the addition and the wound disappears. This is the single most-repeated doctrine in the book — the load-bearing claim from which most of the others follow.
*Appears in: II, IV, V, VII, VIII, IX, X, XI, XII*

### The dichotomy of control
Confine the labels "good" and "bad" to acts of your own will. Health, wealth, honour, dishonour, pain, pleasure, life, death are "middle things" (adiaphora) — the arena in which virtue acts, not goods or evils in themselves. To call any external thing good or evil is to set yourself up for grievance against gods and men.
*Appears in: II, IV, V, VI, VIII, IX, XI*

### Live only in the present moment
The only thing one can ever lose by dying is the present, since past and future are not possessed. The longest and shortest lives are equal in what death takes. Circumscribe each moment, attend to the task in front of you, and refuse to mortgage attention to past regret or future anxiety.
*Appears in: II, III, IV, VII, VIII, XII*

### Death is a natural process, not an evil
Either the gods exist (in which case they place no real evil in your path) or they do not (in which case why cling to a godless world). Death is no more shameful than birth; both are mixtures and resolutions of the same elements. Dying is one more of nature's operations, like teething or aging — and a release from the tyranny of the senses, the errors of the mind, and the servitude of the body.
*Appears in: II, III, IV, VI, VII, VIII, IX, X, XII*

### Death as fair dismissal from the stage
The closing image of the whole work: you have lived as a citizen in the great city of the world; the same nature that brought you in now sends you out, like a praetor fairly dismissing an actor after his appointed acts. "But the play is not yet at an end." Three acts is the whole play. Go your way well pleased and contented, for so is He that dismisses you.
*Appears in: XII (closing meditation)*

### Cosmopolitan citizenship — one body, one law
"My city and country, as I am Antoninus, is Rome; as a man, the whole world." All rational beings share one reason and one law; we are members (not just parts) of one body, made for cooperation as hands, feet, and eyelids cooperate. Opposition to a fellow human is opposition to nature itself.
*Appears in: I, IV, V, VI, VII, VIII, IX, X, XII*

### What hurts the hive hurts the bee
Individual and common good are inseparable. What does not damage the city cannot damage the citizen; what does not benefit the hive cannot benefit the bee. Refer every action to the common good — antisocial action is literally self-amputation, cutting yourself off from the body of mankind. Uniquely, a person can rejoin the whole if he wills it (a mercy granted to no other severed part of nature).
*Appears in: V, VI, VIII, IX, XI*

### Providence or atoms — equanimity under either
Either the universe is providentially ordered, in which case trust the Governor and submit; or it is atoms and chance, in which case use your own providence over what concerns you. Either way, complaint is irrational and tranquillity is available. No metaphysical outcome warrants grasping or grief.
*Appears in: II, IV, VI, VII, IX, X, XII*

### Universal flux — change is the law and the delight of nature
Matter passes like a torrent through particular forms; the cosmos shapes wax now into a horse, then a tree, then a man. To fear change is absurd, since baths, food, and life itself depend on it. Loss and corruption are simply alteration, which is what the nature of the whole most loves.
*Appears in: II, IV, V, VII, IX, X*

### Retreat into yourself, not the countryside
People long for villas, mountains, and seashores, but the truest retreat is into one's own soul. A mind stocked with brief, fundamental precepts can withdraw at any moment, restore tranquillity, and return refreshed. The inner citadel is the strongest fortress; once recollected in herself the mind is unconquerable by fire, iron, tyrant, or tongue.
*Appears in: IV, VII, VIII*

### Strip appearances to their bare nature
A "great juggler" — outward pomp — is most dangerous precisely when one feels engaged in matters of moment. So strip impressions: fine wine is grape juice; the imperial purple is sheep's hair dyed with shellfish blood; sex is friction and convulsion; gold and marble are the earth's "gross faeces"; a body is a bag of blood and corruption. This deflationary gaze cures both desire and fear.
*Appears in: III, VI, VIII, IX, X, XI, XII*

### The view from above
Look down as from a great height on flocks, ships, sacrifices, marriages, courts, fairs, the births and deaths of nations. Asia and Europe are corners; the sea a drop; Mount Athos a clod; all present time a point of eternity. From that vantage, fame and grievance both shrink into visible vanity.
*Appears in: VII, IX, XII*

### Live each act as if it were your last
Every action should be performed with gravity, natural affection, freedom, and justice — and stripped of vanity, hypocrisy, self-love, and revolt against fate. Treating each action as one's final action concentrates attention and purifies motive. Perfection is reached when each day is lived as the last, neither feverish nor dulled, free of pretense.
*Appears in: II, III, VII*

### Premeditatio malorum — the morning meditation
Begin each day by telling yourself: today I shall meet meddlers, ingrates, liars, the envious, the unsociable. They are so through ignorance of true good and evil; you cannot be harmed by them, and you cannot rightly be angry with them, since all rational beings share one reason and are made to cooperate. Practiced in the morning, this inoculates against the day's actual frictions.
*Appears in: I (closing fragment), II, V*

### Pity the wrongdoer; the best revenge is not to become like them
People sin because they take their sins to be their good; teach them better, but do not be angry. To the jaundiced, honey tastes bitter — false opinion compels men as truly as bile or poison. The wrongdoer harms only himself, since vice makes him worse than he was. Marcus's most-quoted maxim: *"The best kind of revenge is not to become like unto them."*
*Appears in: Introduction, II, IV, V, VI, VII, IX, X, XI, XII*

### Bear with men's natural defects, then teach
Don't be angry at a man with bad breath — that is just his nature. If he has reason and offends knowingly, use your reason on his: show him his fault and admonish him gently. Meekness, if true and not affected, is unconquerable. If you cannot teach them, bear with them; if you can, do so without ostentation.
*Appears in: V, VII, VIII, IX, XI*

### The obstacle is the way
A great fire consumes obstacles thrown into it and grows from them. The rational mind likewise turns frustrated intentions into new material for virtuous action: "what before was the impediment, is now the principal object of her working; and that which before was in her way, is now her readiest way." Hold all desires with reservation, so the obstacle becomes material for another virtue.
*Appears in: IV, V, VI, VIII*

### Memento mori as moral pressure (and the decay of the mind, not just the body)
Death hangs over you; while you yet live, while you may, be good. The truly Marcian fear is not death itself but the prior decay of the rational faculty — the very instrument by which the Stoic life is even possible can fail before the body does. Hence the urgency: don't defer the philosophic life to imagined future leisure; you will not live to read the moral commentaries you have laid up for old age.
*Appears in: II, III, IV, V, VII, X*

### Fame is empty, posthumous reputation worthless
Praise is a brief noise from a small corner of a small earth, given by mortals about to die concerning someone about to die. Even immortal fame would mean nothing to the dead. Successive events bury old ones as new sand covers the shore. An emerald, gold, justice, and truth keep their colour regardless of who praises them.
*Appears in: II, III, IV, V, VII, VIII, IX, X, XII*

### Virtue is its own reward
The vine bears its grapes and seeks nothing further; the racehorse runs, the bee makes honey, the eye sees, the foot walks — none expects gratitude. To do good and look for recognition or reciprocation is to misunderstand what you were made for. The good action and the good of doing it are one and the same.
*Appears in: V, VII, IX, XI*

### The soul is dyed by its thoughts
"Such as thy thoughts and ordinary cogitations are, such will thy mind be in time. For the soul doth as it were receive its tincture from the fancies, and imaginations." Steep your soul deliberately in good impressions; cultivate gratitude by inventorying the virtues of those you live among (a discipline Book I performs as an extended exercise — recording what each teacher and family member taught him).
*Appears in: Introduction, I, V, VI*

### Examine each impression analytically
For every object that presents itself, ask: What is it in itself? What does it consist of? How long can it last? Which virtue does this present situation call for — meekness, fortitude, truth, sincerity? What is its cause — God, fate, coincidence, or a neighbour acting in ignorance? Decompose admired pleasures (a song into separate notes, a dance into separate motions) and they lose their grip.
*Appears in: III, VII, VIII, X, XI, XII*

### Reform your prayer
If the gods can do nothing, why pray; if they can, ask not for outward things but for freedom from lust and fear. Don't pray to obtain the woman, the troublesome companion, or the unsold child — pray to be without the desire, the impatience, or the fear in the first place.
*Appears in: IX*

### Keep the dogmata ready, like a surgeon's tools
Settled principles only retain power if continually refreshed. Carry them like a pancratiast — with both hands and feet free, integrated and redundant — not like a gladiator who is undone if he loses his sword. A philosopher should be able to be killed by men but never disarmed.
*Appears in: III, VII, XII*

### Asking for help is no shame
If reason suffices, use it without ostentation; if not, hand the work on or do it jointly. Like a lame soldier scaling a wall, do not abandon a public good simply because you cannot accomplish it alone. The criterion is always whether the work serves the common benefit, not personal credit.
*Appears in: VII*

### Be the man, do not discuss the man
"Make it not any longer a matter of dispute or discourse, what are the signs and proprieties of a good man, but really and actually to be such." If others cannot bear a man living truly as a man, let them kill him — better to die than live as they would have you live.
*Appears in: X, XI, XII*

### The threefold self — only the ruling reason is properly "you"
A human being is flesh (knit-work of nerves, veins, skin), animating breath, and the ruling rational part. The first two must be cared for but not identified with; only the third is properly "you" and must be guarded. The body's instruments — eyes, hands, feet — are inert as a carpenter's axe lying alone or a shuttle without a weaver, until moved by the inward dogma.
*Appears in: I, II, X, XII*

## Chapter Map

**Introduction (George Long, 1862).** Long frames the *Meditations* as private notes, not a treatise — Marcus writing to ease his own heart in camp before the Quadi. Sketches Marcus's biography (born 121 A.D., adopted by Antoninus Pius, co-emperor with the dissolute Lucius Verus, plagued by Marcomannic wars, plague, famine, the Avidius Cassius revolt, dies in Pannonia 180), then lays out the Stoic system Marcus inherits — Zeno's founding, Chrysippus's systematizing, the materialist pantheism, the doctrine of "indifferents," the empiricist epistemology of *kataleptike phantasia*. Sustained Victorian comparison with Thomas à Kempis's *Imitation of Christ* delineates what is and is not Christian about Marcus's spirituality. Calls his treatment of the Christians "the great blot" on his name.

**Book I.** Unlike anything else in the *Meditations*: a catalogue of Marcus's debts to specific people, virtue by virtue. From his grandfather Verus, gentleness; from his mother, piety and contentment with little; from Diognetus, freedom from superstition and the turn to philosophy; from Rusticus, the gift of Epictetus's *Discourses* and the practice of unaffected character; from Apollonius, steadfastness in pain and illness; from his adoptive father Antoninus Pius (the longest entry), the picture of a ruler who is meek without weakness, sober without austerity, and "knew, like Socrates, both how to lack and how to enjoy." Closes with a thanksgiving to the gods and the famous morning meditation written "in the country of the Quadi at Granua."

**Book II.** A self-addressed manual written at Carnuntum on the Marcomannic frontier. Tight loop between three themes: brevity of life, indifference of externals, and the rationality of accepting nature's order. Contains the dichotomy argument about death (gods or no gods, equanimity follows), the doctrine that we can only ever lose the present, and the closing definition of philosophy as preserving the inner *daimon* against pain, pleasure, and pretense — culminating in calm cheerfulness toward death.

**Book III.** Dominated by urgency: not just mortality but the prior decay of the rational faculty. The good man as a "priest and minister of the gods" tending the inner temple. Methodological — examine each impression in its bare nature, ask what virtue it calls for, keep dogmata ready like a surgeon's tools. Closes by sharply distinguishing the good man from the merely rational man: atheists and traitors also follow reason for ordinary duties; what marks the good man is glad acceptance of providence and unwavering allegiance to truth and justice even when unwitnessed.

**Book IV.** The densest concentration of core doctrines — almost a Stoic catechism. Three interlocking themes: the sovereignty of the inner ruling faculty, the indifference of externals (harm comes only through opinion), and universal flux that sweeps away bodies, names, empires, and even the rememberers. Contains the classic images of the inner retreat ("a man cannot any whither retire better than to his own soul"), the promontory standing firm against the sea, and the ripe olive falling and praising the ground that bore it.

**Book V.** Practical and cohesive. Opens with the famous reveille: "In the morning when thou findest thyself unwilling to rise, consider with thyself presently, it is to go about a man's work that I am stirred up." Sparrows, ants, spiders, and bees rush to their natural function — so should you. Develops the analogy of fate as a physician's prescription (illness, blindness, loss are prescribed for the health of the whole), and contains the seed of the "obstacle becomes the way" teaching. Closes with a definition of happiness as self-administered virtue.

**Book VI.** Cosmological, axiological, therapeutic. Matter is pliable, the governing reason has no evil in it, so trusting the order of things is rational. Contains the famous trilemma (providence / atoms / intermediate) used not as metaphysics for its own sake but to show that no metaphysical outcome warrants grasping. Cosmopolitan: "My city and country, as I am Antoninus, is Rome; as a man, the whole world." Long portrait of Antoninus Pius as the book's ethical centerpiece — the philosophic life lived inside a court.

**Book VII.** Programmatic and aphoristic — many entries function as standalone maxims. Drills three doctrines: one cosmos governed by one reason; the *hegemonikon* harmed only by its own assents; rational creatures as members of one body owing each other gentleness. Draws on Plato, Antisthenes, the comics, even Epicurus on pain — pressing other schools into Stoic service. Famous images: life as a wrestler's practice (not a dancer's); "look within; within is the fountain of all good"; new sand burying old on the shore.

**Book VIII.** Self-addressed and dense with imagery. Opens with the frank admission that Marcus has missed being counted a lifelong philosopher and that this very fact should liberate him from caring about reputation. Three disciplines: assent (refuse to add opinion to bare impressions), action (every act referred to the common good), desire (welcome whatever the universal nature assigns as material for virtue). Memorable images: the tossed ball, the unmuddied fountain, the sunbeam through a narrow hole, the carpenter's shavings, the bitter cucumber.

**Book IX.** The most theologically argumentative book. Reframes ordinary moral failings — injustice, lying, hedonism, fear of pain — as forms of impiety against the universal nature, "the most ancient and venerable of all the deities." Strikingly therapeutic: meet death as you would the birth of a child; look down on human affairs from a great height; reform prayer so that it asks for the absence of desire and fear rather than for goods. Closes with the image of doing good without recompense — the eye that simply sees, the foot that simply walks.

**Book X.** Marcus's most intimate book. Opens with a prayer-like address to his own soul ("O my soul, the time I trust will be, when thou shalt be good, simple, single..."). Builds an arsenal of meditative exercises against the daily erosion of "toys and fooleries at home, wars abroad" — the morning judgment, the cosmic shrinking lens, the catalogue of dead courts, the image of leaves blown down and budding. Sharp social edges: the conqueror as a more pretentious spider; the pig squealing as its throat is cut as the figure of a man bewailing worldly losses.

**Book XI.** The most pedagogically systematic. Opens with a portrait of the rational soul as self-completing, social, and identical with justice. Then a toolkit: analytic decomposition of pleasures, readiness for death without showmanship, the branch-and-tree image of belonging. Centerpiece is the famous nine-headed therapy for anger at others' wrongdoing (plus a tenth "gift from Hercules"). Latter third shifts genre into a commonplace book — Socrates anecdotes, Pythagorean morning customs, direct quotations from Epictetus on free will.

**Book XII.** A closing book. No new doctrine; instead, gathering, restating, tightening — the threefold self, the analysis into matter/form/end, the trilemma, the harbor of opinion, the kinship of rational minds. Tone valedictory and increasingly intimate. The center of gravity is death; the final section supplies the most memorable image in the *Meditations* — the praetor dismissing the actor from the stage with three acts as the whole play. Closing words: "Go thy ways then well pleased and contented: for so is He that dismisseth thee."

**Appendix (Long on the Fronto correspondence).** Long uses the Marcus–Fronto letters (recovered from a palimpsest in 1815) to humanize the philosopher-emperor: rising before dawn to study, hunting with Antoninus Pius, gathering grapes at vintage, killing a scorpion in his bedclothes. Long is dismissive of Fronto as a stylist — "the fount of Marcus's eloquence was pumped up by artificial means" — but elevates him as a moral presence: a rare Roman courtier capable of frank speech and disinterested affection. Notes the strange asymmetry: in the *Meditations* Marcus credits Fronto with only one lesson, that "affection" is not a native Roman quality. What endured was not the pedagogy but the human warmth.

**Notes (editor's apparatus).** Text-critical and philological scaffolding: emendations Casaubon (1634) proposed, source identifications for Marcus's quotations (Homer, Hesiod, Plato, Euripides, Sophocles, Epictetus, Plutarch), and corrections where Casaubon's English misreads the Greek. No substantive philosophical content.

## Notable Quotes

> "The best kind of revenge is, not to become like unto them."
> — *Introduction & VI* — The most-cited maxim in the *Meditations*: meet wrongdoing with self-mastery, not retaliation.

> "Such as thy thoughts and ordinary cogitations are, such will thy mind be in time."
> — *Introduction & V* — On the formative power of habitual thought.

> "Every man's happiness depends from himself, but behold thy life is almost at an end, whiles affording thyself no respect, thou dost make thy happiness to consist in the souls, and conceits of other men."
> — *II* — The Stoic locus of happiness, delivered as self-rebuke.

> "Death hangs over thee: whilst yet thou livest, whilst thou mayest, be good."
> — *IV* — Mortality as moral pressure rather than morbid weight.

> "In the morning when thou findest thyself unwilling to rise, consider with thyself presently, it is to go about a man's work that I am stirred up."
> — *V (opening)* — Daily life framed as return to one's proper function rather than to comfort.

> "What before was the impediment, is now the principal object of her working; and that which before was in her way, is now her readiest way."
> — *V* — The classic "obstacle is the way" formulation.

> "My city and country, as I am Antoninus, is Rome; as a man, the whole world."
> — *VI* — Cosmopolitan citizenship: the local political identity is real but subordinate to membership in the cosmic commonwealth.

> "Look within; within is the fountain of all good. Such a fountain, where springing waters can never fail, so thou dig still deeper and deeper."
> — *VII* — The remedy for affliction is not external rearrangement but inward labour.

> "Let thy chief fort and place of defence be, a mind free from passions."
> — *VIII* — The inner citadel doctrine.

> "All men are made one for another: either then teach them better, or bear with them."
> — *VIII* — Marcus's compact social ethic.

> "In sum, either there is a God, and then all is well; or if all things go by chance and fortune, yet mayest thou use thine own providence in those things that concern thee properly; and then art thou well."
> — *IX* — The providence-or-atoms disjunction.

> "One prayeth that he may compass his desire, to lie with such or such a one, pray thou that thou mayst not lust to lie with her."
> — *IX* — Reformed prayer: ask not for outward things but for freedom from passions.

> "Whatsoever is reasonable, is also sociable."
> — *X* — The lapidary equation grounding Stoic ethics.

> "Make it not any longer a matter of dispute or discourse, what are the signs and proprieties of a good man, but really and actually to be such."
> — *X* — Aphorism rebuking philosophical chatter in favour of practice.

> "Cast away from thee opinion, and thou art safe. And what is it that hinders thee from casting of it away?"
> — *XII* — The central Stoic move: distress is a function of judgment, not event.

> "As if the praetor should fairly dismiss him from the stage, whom he had taken in to act a while... Go thy ways then well pleased and contented: for so is He that dismisseth thee."
> — *XII (closing)* — The image that closes the entire *Meditations*: death as fair dismissal from the play of life.
